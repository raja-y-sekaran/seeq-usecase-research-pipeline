Cement and building materials

Induced-draft fan maintenance (cement)

Problem. In a cement plant, induced-draft (ID) fans pull gas through the kiln, creating the draft that speeds up drying of the clinker. Over time dust scale builds up on the fan blades, which raises vibration until the fan can break down. A large US building materials supplier had about one full day of unplanned ID fan downtime per quarter, at about $20,000 per hour, nearly $500,000 in total. Routine scheduled cleaning can also be unnecessary and expensive, and spreadsheets were too slow to predict failures. Data available: fan vibration and process data in Wonderware Historian and OSIsoft PI.

Solution. Forecast the vibration and clean the blades just before it gets too high. Seeq filters the vibration signal with an agile filter, and the Prediction tool models vibration against time since the last maintenance. The forecast shows when vibration will reach the critical limit, so cleaning can be planned. The model updates as new data arrives, and overlaying the current cycle on past cycles helps troubleshoot vibration events.

  1. Data connectors Fan vibration, fan speed or status, in one worksheet.
  2. Formula (agile filter) Agile filter with a window such as 15 minutes.
    $vibration.agileFilter(15min)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  3. Value Search Speed below 10 rpm for at least 4 hours (a maintenance stop).
    $fanSpeed.valueSearch(3d, isLessThan(10), 4h)Confirmed in Seeq docs
  4. Composite Condition From the end of one cleaning to the start of the next.
  5. Formula Days since the end of the last cleaning stop, restarting at zero after each one.
    timeSince($cleaningStop)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  6. Prediction Train on past Operating Cycles (fan running only); linear or simple curve.
  7. Formula Space left to the limit divided by the rise per day.
    ($criticalLimit - $vibration) / $risePerDayConfirmed in Seeq docs
  8. Value Search Below, for example, 7 days.
    $daysToLimit.valueSearch(30d, isLessThan(7))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  9. Capsule Time view Overlay the current cycle on past cycles from cycle start.
  10. Organizer Topic Shared with reliability, maintenance scheduling and plant management.

Kiln stoppage root cause analysis (cement)

Problem. A large US building materials company had unexplained cement kiln stoppages. Unplanned stoppages usually happen about once a quarter, but once there were two multi-hour stoppages in five days. Each hour of stoppage costs about $20,000 in lost production. A kiln can stop for many reasons: an induced-draft fan bearing failure, high pressure from a blockage, or chemical incompatibility. Engineers tried to find the causes with Excel charts and reports, which was cumbersome and did not get to the bottom of it. Data available: process data in a Wonderware historian.

Solution. Let Seeq find every stoppage and line them up for comparison. The kiln feed flow is smoothed with the agile filter, and Value Search marks each stoppage as a capsule when feed flow is low. Engineers zoom into the time just before each stoppage and compare stoppages side by side to see whether the same cause keeps coming back. An Organizer Topic updates automatically and tells operators about new stoppages.

  1. Signal Smoothing (agile filter) Agile filter with a window of a few minutes.
  2. Value Search Feed below a low level (for example 5 t/h) for at least 15 minutes.

    $kilnStoppage.beforeStart(2h)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified

  5. Signal from Condition Duration in hours per stoppage.

Free lime soft sensor (cement)

Problem. Free lime (unreacted lime left in the clinker) is a key quality number in cement making; most plants aim for 1 % to 2 %. The page says a high reading means raw materials and energy are wasted, while a low reading points to quality problems that can cost revenue and reputation. Free lime is measured by sending a clinker sample to the lab, and the result takes at least an hour to reach the historian. That delay makes it hard for operators to tune the process, and alternative fuels such as shredded tyres and municipal waste make free lime even harder to control. Data available: process data in Wonderware and OSIsoft PI; lab results in a laboratory data management system.

Solution. Build a 'soft sensor': a model that estimates free lime every minute from the NOx signal, which is measured continuously. Seeq smooths both signals with the agile filter, marks the training period with a condition, and uses the Prediction tool with NOx as input. The model updates automatically, so the operator sees near real-time free lime and can adjust fuel to cut waste while keeping clinker quality good.

    $freeLime.move(-1h)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified

Oil & gas (upstream)

Electrical submersible pump (ESP) up-time

Problem. Many oil wells do not have enough reservoir pressure to push fluid to the surface, so an electrical submersible pump (ESP) down the well lifts it. These pumps do not always run well, and a sudden loss of flow can damage the pump. That means lost production and an expensive repair. Simple trip logic can also stop pumps too early and lose production. The problem covers hundreds of pads and thousands of wells, so before Seeq, finding and analysing ESP failures was purely reactive. Data available: wellhead temperature, motor current (amps) and tubing choke data in OSIsoft PI, with wells organised in OSIsoft Asset Framework.

Solution. Detect low-flow events from signals at the surface. When flow drops, the wellhead temperature drops, and motor amps and choke data help confirm it. One or two conditions alone were not accurate, so Seeq combines 15 conditions. Low-flow events during well tests or maintenance are ignored. Seeq also builds precursor capsules that warn before a low-flow event. The analytic is set up on historical events, then watches live data, and the asset model copies it to every well.

  1. Signal Smoothing Agile or moving-average filter over about 10 minutes.
    $wellheadTemp.agileFilter(10min)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  2. Value Search One search per state, for example amps less than 5 for pump off.
    $ampsSmooth.valueSearch(1day, isLessThan(5))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  3. Value Search (and Profile Search for shapes) For example: temperature below a well-specific limit for at least 5 minutes; amps well below normal; choke not recently closed by the operator. Profile Search can catch a typical temperature-drop shape.
    $tempSmooth.valueSearch(1day, isLessThan(55), 5min)Confirmed in Seeq docs
  4. Composite Condition (or Formula) Intersection (A and B) so the clues must agree. Use several Composite Conditions in a row, or one formula.
    $cond1.intersect($cond2).intersect($cond3)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  5. Composite Condition A outside B (subtract the times to ignore).
    $rawLowFlow.subtract($wellTest).subtract($maintenance)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  6. Trend view and Capsule Time Line up the events at their start; show data before the start.
  7. Formula, then Value Search on what you learn First mark the hour before each past event; study what the signals do there; then build a search for that early pattern, such as a slow temperature fall with unsteady amps.
    $lowFlowEvent.beforeStart(1h)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  8. Asset tree (OSIsoft Asset Framework) Build once on one well with asset-relative signals, then swap or roll up across all wells.
  9. Scorecard Metric and Organizer Topic Colour thresholds; auto-updating Topic.

Field-wide analysis of Coriolis meter drive gain

Problem. In an oil field, separators split oil, water and gas. When separation goes wrong, gas can escape with the liquid (gas blowby) or liquid can go out with the gas (liquid carryover). Engineers need to know quickly so they can fix it. Coriolis flow meters are common because they cope with sand, debris and gas coming out of the liquid. Their Drive Gain (the percentage of input power the meter needs to keep its tubes vibrating) rises when there is gas in the liquid or the meter is badly calibrated. One engineer may have hundreds of meters per area to watch, so checking them by hand is not possible. Data available: meter data, including Drive Gain, from Ignition SCADA.

Solution. Let Seeq find the bad meters every morning. A Python script using the Seeq SDK finds all the Drive Gain tags for an area in the asset tree. Value Search marks every time Drive Gain is above 60 %, Signal from Condition adds up how long that lasted, and the meters are ranked by hours above 60 % on the previous day. The script runs every morning and makes a new set of ranked worksheets, worst meter at the top.

  1. Seeq Python SDK (in Seeq Data Lab) Search the area for signals named Drive Gain.
    spy.search({'Path': 'Area 1', 'Name': 'Drive Gain'})Illustrative
  2. Value Search Greater than 60 %. Maximum capsule duration 1 day; ignore very short blips if wanted.
    $driveGain.valueSearch(1day, isGreaterThan(60))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  3. Periodic Condition Daily, starting at midnight in the field's time zone.
  4. Signal from Condition Statistic: total duration, in hours. Timestamp at the end of the day.
  5. Scorecard Metric (table) Sort from most to least hours; colour, for example, red above 4 h, yellow 1 to 4 h, green below 1 h.
  6. Seeq Python SDK (scheduled) Schedule the script each morning to make new ranked worksheets, one per offending meter.

Emergency shutdown (ESD) water hammer characterization

Problem. Oil collection networks have emergency shutdown (ESD) systems that close valves fast to protect the plant. But a fast valve closure suddenly stops the moving liquid and sends a pressure wave back and forth through the pipe. This is water hammer. Water hammer can damage pipes and vessels and blow relief valves or rupture disks, with safety, reliability and environmental impacts. Operators wanted to find where it happens so they could redesign the ESD valves and equipment. Before Seeq the analysis was purely reactive, because there were too many wells and too much data for normal tools. Data available: well pressure and ESD data in OSIsoft PI, with wells organised in OSIsoft Asset Framework.

Solution. Look for the fingerprint of water hammer right after each ESD trip: pressure peaks that repeat at a steady interval and get smaller each time. Seeq calculates the slope of the pressure, marks each local peak, checks that the time between peaks is steady (Value Search), and measures how big the swings are (Signal from Condition). The asset model copies this to every well, so the worst-hit wells can be found and fixed.

  1. Value Search Valve position below 5 % (closed). Maximum capsule duration 1 day.
    $esdValve.valueSearch(1day, isLessThan(5))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  2. Signal Smoothing A very short window (a few seconds) so noise goes but peaks stay.
    $pressure.agileFilter(10s)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  3. Formula Rate of change of pressure.
    $pressureClean.derivative()Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  4. Value Search Slope greater than 0 gives 'rising' capsules; the end of each rising capsule is a local peak. Mark it with a sample of the pressure there.
    $slope.valueSearch(1h, isGreaterThan(0))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  5. Formula and Value Search Time between each peak and the next; keep runs where this stays within a narrow band (for example 2 to 3 s).
  6. Composite Condition Overlap (intersection).
    $periodicPeaks.intersect($esdTrip.grow(2min))Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  7. Signal from Condition Maximum and range per event; also the ratio of each peak to the one before.
  8. Asset tree (OSIsoft Asset Framework) and Scorecard Metric Build on one well, then swap across the tree; table of event count and largest swing per well.

Electric utilities (T&D)

Energy storage capital expense optimization (where to put batteries)

Problem. Each substation in a transmission and distribution (T&D) network has several transformers feeding local circuits. A substation's capacity is usually the sum of its transformer ratings, and utilities must report to regulators when load goes over capacity. So a utility can compare total MVA load with the total normal rating and the 24-hour emergency rating. To avoid overloads, utilities install batteries. Batteries are expensive, so the location matters. The choice depends on whether the transformers can serve today's peak, whether the others can carry the whole station if one fails, asset health, outage impact, forecast demand and battery cost. Data available: load data in PI; transformer capacity ratings in a SQL database.

Solution. Add up the transformer loads, smooth them, and compare them with the normal and emergency ratings. Ask 'what if one transformer had failed?' by checking whether the remaining transformer could have carried the whole load. Measure how often and how long limits would be exceeded, and repeat the same analysis for every substation with asset swapping, so batteries go where the risk of overload is highest.

  1. Formula Add the transformer loads together.
    $loadT1 + $loadT2Confirmed in Seeq docs
  2. Signal Smoothing (Low Pass Filter) Low-pass filter with a window such as 10 to 15 minutes; also remove outliers, downtime and abnormal data.
  3. SQL connector + Formula Load the ratings as signals or scalars per transformer and per station.
  4. Periodic Condition Daily, in local time.
  5. Formula (splice) During the simulated outage, splice in a limit equal to T2's emergency rating instead of the whole station's.
    $stationRating.splice($t2Emergency, $t1Out)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  6. Deviation Search / Value Search Load is above the limit; maximum capsule 1 day.
    $smoothedLoad.valueSearch(1day, isGreaterThan(30))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  7. Signal from Condition Total Duration (hours) and Maximum overload (MVA) per day.
  8. Scorecard Metric + asset swapping + Treemap Colour thresholds (for example red above 2 hours a day); swap the worksheet across the asset tree.

True transformer age for replacement

Problem. Deciding when to replace a substation power transformer is hard. Manufacturers usually call 40 years the end of life, but a lightly loaded transformer may not reach a true age of 40 until its 60th or 70th year in service. Working out how loading above the nameplate rating has aged a transformer over many years is very difficult without the right analytics, so it is almost impossible to know its true age and the right time to replace it. Data available: load, temperature and oil/gas analysis data in PI; equipment heat-run properties (from factory tests) in a SQL database.

Solution. Use the IEEE C57.91-2011 standard, which gives an aging acceleration factor for a given load and temperature and the percent loss of life of the insulation. In Seeq, combine live temperature and load data with each transformer's nameplate and heat-run data, calculate how fast it is ageing at every moment, add that up over time to get its true age, and compare transformers across the fleet.

  1. Asset tree + SQL and PI connectors Attach each transformer's signals and nameplate values to its node in the asset tree.
  2. Formula Hot-spot = top-oil + rated rise × (load ratio)^m (or use a measured winding temperature if there is one).
    $topOil + $ratedRise * ($load / $ratedLoad)^$mIdea confirmed, syntax unverified
  3. Formula F_AA = exp(15000/383 − 15000/(hot-spot + 273)). Equals 1 at 110 °C; above 1 means faster ageing.
    exp(15000/383 - 15000/($hotSpot + 273))Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  4. Periodic Condition + Signal from Condition Average F_AA × 24 h (or the time integral of F_AA) per day.
  5. Formula Sum the equivalent hours and divide by 8,760 to get years; add the age before the data began (extrapolated to installation year). Percent loss of life = equivalent hours ÷ normal insulation life × 100.
  6. Value Search F_AA greater than 1, or hot-spot above 110 °C.
    $faa.valueSearch(1day, isGreaterThan(1))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  7. Scatterplot Colour points by ambient temperature.
  8. Scorecard + asset swapping + Treemap Colour by true age against calendar age; swap the worksheet across all transformers.

Continuous drug product Quality by Design (QbD) modeling

Problem. Drug makers must keep critical process parameters, such as temperature or agitation, inside required ranges. If one is out of range, the batch fails, which can mean millions of dollars of rework and lost revenue. Quality by Design (QbD) looks at several variables and how they interact. Using models from experiments, one variable may go wider if the others are in range: for example a 5-degree temperature deviation may be fine for quality if the rest are within limits. This expands the design space and cuts the number of major or critical deviations, each of which can cost tens of thousands of dollars. When a product moves from R&D to a commercial plant, much of the design knowledge is lost through different equipment and people. A major drug maker wanted to improve its QbD modelling in R&D and carry that knowledge into production. Data available: lab results in Microsoft SQL, process data in OSIsoft PI.

Solution. Take the Design of Experiments (DOE) data from a continuous wet granulation step. Shift each signal in time according to where its sensor sits on the line, keep only steady-state operation, and build a model in Seeq's Prediction tool for the critical quality attributes (CQAs). Then apply that multivariate design space to commercial production as a boundary for continuous monitoring, flagging any deviation as a capsule.

  1. Formula (time shift) Shift each signal by the travel time from its sensor to the reference point.
    $feederRate.move(45s)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified
  2. Value Search + Composite Condition Each key signal steady inside its DOE set range for a few minutes; combine with AND.
    $feedRate.valueSearch(1day, isBetween(10, 12), 2min)Confirmed in Seeq docs
  3. SQL connector Load as a signal with a sample at each test time.
  4. Prediction Training window = the DOE runs; Limit to condition = Steady State; OLS, or Ridge/PCA if inputs move together; keep terms with p-value ≤ 0.05.
  5. Formula / Value Search Predicted CQA outside its specification range.
    $cqaPredicted.valueSearch(1day, isGreaterThan(220))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  6. Capsule Time view + Scorecard Metric Overlay deviations from their start; table of count and total duration.

Crystallization deviation investigation (pharma)

Problem. Small-molecule drugs are made in batches. The last step turns the product into solid crystals, which are then filtered. During a commercial campaign, filtration took longer than the specification allows, in one batch more than twice as long. That is a deviation and a sign of a possible quality problem. A deviation can threaten the whole batch. Batch failures cost millions, and restarting can take days or weeks while the team proves it understands what happened. Spreadsheets were too slow: trend differences are hard to see, the energy balance on the crystallization profile is hard to calculate in Excel, and every new time period meant rebuilding the calculations. Data available: batch process data in OSIsoft PI, including jacket temperature, contents temperature and a noisy level signal.

Solution. Measure how much heat each batch gives off while it crystallizes. Different crystal forms (polymorphs) release different amounts of heat. Seeq finds the crystallization and filtration phases, works out the jacket-to-contents temperature difference, and adds it up (integrates it) over the crystallization phase. The slow-filtering batch had a clearly different heat integral, which showed it had formed the wrong polymorph. The process was then changed so the desired polymorph forms every time.

  1. Seeq data connection (OSIsoft PI) No special settings. Seeq lines up signals that are recorded at different intervals.
  2. Signal Smoothing Low-pass or agile filter over about 10 minutes.
    $level.agileFilter(10min)Idea confirmed, syntax unverified

They cause corrosion and foul or plug trays, exchangers and pipes, which can lead to loss of containment, safety risks and lost money. Engineers need to know when the unit runs in a high-risk condition, shown as a continuous signal of 'percent of time at risk'. Before Seeq this meant combining lab and process data from separate systems in a spreadsheet, taking hours or days. Data available: process temperatures in a historian (OSIsoft PI, PHD or others) and lab results for H2S, NH3 and HCl in a SQL database.

Solution. Bring lab and process data together in Seeq, remove shutdowns and start-ups, and use Formula to calculate the salt deposition temperature (SDT) of each salt from the lab results. Compare the stream temperature with the SDT plus a safety margin; Deviation Search marks every at-risk period, and the result is trended as percent of time at risk and shown as a histogram.

Refining and utilities

Minimize vented steam in a dual boiler system

Problem. Many plants make their steam with two boilers. A boiler cannot turn down below a minimum firing rate. In warm months the plant needs less steam, so with both boilers at minimum they still make more steam than is used, and the extra is vented to the air. Vented steam is wasted fuel, money and CO2. Running on one boiler would stop the venting, but if that single boiler trips, the plant can lose production. Managers need numbers to weigh the saving against that risk. Data available: boiler firing rates or steam flows, vent flow or vent valve position, and outside air temperature, from the plant historian. The Seeq page does not name the historian or the tags.

Solution. Find the times when both boilers sit at minimum firing and steam is still being vented, and add up the vented steam over a year to get its cost. Then use history to estimate how often a single boiler would trip, and multiply by what a trip costs (probability × financial consequence). If the steam saving is clearly bigger than the risk, idle one boiler in warm weather.

  1. Value Search + Composite Condition One Value Search per boiler: firing rate less than or equal to its minimum (for example 20 %). Combine with Intersection (both at once). Ignore short gaps so small wobbles don't split events.
    $b1Firing.valueSearch(1day, isLessThanOrEqualTo(20))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  2. Value Search Flow is greater than a small noise level, for example 0.5 t/h.
    $ventFlow.valueSearch(1day, isGreaterThan(0.5))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  3. Composite Condition Intersection (A and B).
  4. Signal from Condition Statistic: Totalized. Make sure the flow tag has a unit (t/h or kg/h) so the total comes out in tonnes.
  5. Periodic Condition Yearly, or monthly to see the warm-weather pattern.
  6. Signal from Condition + Formula Sum the tonnes in each year, then multiply by the cost per tonne.
    $tonnesPerYear * $costPerTonneConfirmed in Seeq docs
  7. Value Search + Signal from Condition Value Search for a sudden drop to zero while the boiler should be running; Signal from Condition with statistic Count per year.
  8. Formula Risk = probability × cost of a trip. Net benefit = steam saving − risk.
    $steamCost - ($tripProbability * $costPerTrip)Confirmed in Seeq docs

Emissions reporting during detector exceedance events

Problem. Plants that emit certain pollutants must report them to environmental regulators under strict rules. When the measuring instrument goes down or goes 'out of range' (stuck at its maximum reading), the real pollutant level must be estimated and reported within a tight deadline. These calculations were complex and done by hand, so an engineer had to drop everything (often at night) to do them. Data available: detector and process data in a historian such as OSIsoft PI, Honeywell PHD or AspenTech IP21.

Solution. Let Seeq detect each exceedance event and do the estimate automatically. It removes readings outside the detector's range, finds when the detector is pegged at its limit, uses the rate of change just before and after to model the hidden part of the signal, and calculates the maximum concentration and the total mass released above the detector limit. An auto-updating Organizer Topic shares each event's results as a link or PDF.

  1. Value Search At or above the detector maximum, for example 500 ppm.
    $so2.valueSearch(1d, isGreaterThanOrEqualTo(500))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  2. Formula Remove the flat, pegged readings.
  3. Formula Rate of change (derivative), in ppm per minute.
    $cleanSo2.derivative()Idea confirmed, syntax unverified

  5. Formula Treat the hidden part as a triangle: height above the limit = rise × fall × duration ÷ (rise + fall).
    $riseRate * $fallRate * $duration / ($riseRate + $fallRate)Confirmed in Seeq docs

  7. Formula (Modelled − limit) × flow × factor (kg per m³ per ppm).

Renewable power (solar)

Solar plant: axis tracking and soiling losses

Problem. A solar plant makes less power when its panels get dirty (soiling) or when the trackers that turn the panels to follow the sun stop following their setpoint. Plants usually use a flat, assumed loss number, based on weather and total output, to decide when cleaning panels or fixing trackers is worth it. Building a proper model of these losses was hard, or needed expensive special software that did not allow tuning or quick one-off analysis. Data available: tracker positions and setpoints, plant output and meteorology instruments, in a Wonderware historian.

Solution. Find the times when the plant was at its best: panels clean and trackers following their setpoints. Use those times to train a model of how much power the plant should make from the weather readings. The gap between the model and actual output is the loss. Loss during tracker faults is tracking loss; the rest is mostly soiling. Turn the loss into dollars and compare it with the cost of cleaning or repair.

  1. Formula Absolute difference between position and setpoint.
    ($position - $setpoint).abs()Idea confirmed, syntax unverified

    $trackingError.valueSearch(1day, isGreaterThan(3))Confirmed in Seeq docs
  3. Value Search Irradiance above a small value, for example 200 W/m².
    $irradiance.valueSearch(1day, isGreaterThan(200))Confirmed in Seeq docs

    $sunny.subtract($faultString1.union($faultString2))Idea confirmed, syntax unverified

Renewable power (wind)

Wind turbine curtailment and ramp-up losses

Problem. Grid operators sometimes 'curtail' wind farms: the wind is there, but the power is not taken, because demand is low or the lines are congested. The wind company may charge the utility for revenue lost during curtailment. But after a shutdown a wind turbine takes six to eight hours to ramp back up to full output, and that time also loses money. Spreadsheets could not pick out curtailment and ramp-up periods accurately, especially across a fleet. One wind company had curtailments about every two days, costing several hundred dollars a day, roughly $3,000 per month per wind farm, and could not quantify it. Data available: OSIsoft PI and MySQL process data, organised with PI Asset Framework.

Solution. Work out how much power each turbine could have made from the wind speed and direction (its generation potential). Mark curtailment periods, then mark the ramp-up from the end of each curtailment until the turbine reaches its potential again. Compare potential with actual power in those windows, add up the lost energy for the chargeback, and run the same analysis across hundreds of turbines with PI Asset Framework.

  1. Prediction (or Formula with the turbine's power curve) Train only on online, uncurtailed periods; use a polynomial or power-curve shape, since power rises steeply with wind speed.
  2. Value Search Curtailed status, or a power shortfall above a threshold; maximum capsule 1 day.
  3. Value Search options + Composite Condition Ignore curtailments shorter than a set time; subtract times the turbine itself was offline.
  4. Composite Condition / Formula From the end of each curtailment until actual power gets back to its potential (for example within 95 %).
  5. Formula Potential minus actual, never below zero.
    $potential - $actualConfirmed in Seeq docs
  6. Signal from Condition + Scorecard Metric Totalized (MWh) per event and per month; multiply by the contract price.
  7. PI Asset Framework + asset swapping Swap across every turbine and wind farm.
